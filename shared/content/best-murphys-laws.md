# Best Murphy's Laws

The best Murphy's Laws are short, specific, and painfully reusable. They name a failure pattern clearly enough that people recognize it before the punchline lands.

## Start here

- If anything can go wrong, it will.
- The backup you need most is the one you forgot to test.
- The task that looks simple is hiding the missing requirement.

## The classics

A handful of laws have lasted because each one describes a different way plans fail.

- **Murphy's Law.** Anything that can go wrong will go wrong. It came out of a 1949 Air Force test program, and the [origin story](/origin-story) explains how the saying got its name.
- **Sod's Law.** Whatever can go wrong will, and at the worst possible moment. It is the British cousin of Murphy's Law, compared in [Murphy's Law vs Sod's Law](/murphys-law-vs-sods-law).
- **Hofstadter's Law.** It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law. Douglas Hofstadter coined it in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979), and it is the best argument against trusting your own estimates.
- **Parkinson's Law.** Work expands to fill the time available for its completion. C. Northcote Parkinson published it in *The Economist* in 1955.
- **Brooks's Law.** Adding people to a late software project makes it later. Fred Brooks argued this in *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975).

## Why these work

Each law captures a repeatable situation, not a one-time accident. That makes it useful for planning, debugging, and explaining why defensive design matters.

The best ones share three traits. They are short enough to remember, specific enough to picture, and true often enough that you can check them against your own week. A law that fails any of those tests tends to disappear.

## How to use them

Treat a good law as a checklist item rather than a joke. Before a launch, ask which of these patterns applies: Is there an untested backup? Is the estimate optimistic? Is a requirement still missing? Naming the pattern early is cheaper than discovering it late.
